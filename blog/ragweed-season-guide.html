Ragweed season, explained

Guide · updated August 2026

Ragweed (Ambrosia) is the dominant late-summer allergy trigger across much of the eastern and midwestern United States. If your symptoms reliably arrive in the second half of August and hang on into autumn, ragweed is the usual suspect.

The season, roughly

  • Starts: mid-August in much of the Northeast and Midwest.
  • Peaks: the second or third week of September.
  • Ends: at the first hard frost.

Timing shifts with latitude and weather — it begins earlier in the south and later further north, and a warm autumn extends it. Treat any published date as an average, not a promise.

It is not the only weed in the air

Ragweed gets the headlines, but late-summer weed pollen is a mix. Counts published by New York-area counting stations in August 2026 regularly listed nettle as the single most common grain, alongside ragweed, lambs quarters, plantain and asters.

That matters because an app that reports only "weed pollen" cannot tell you which of those you are reacting to. Pollenary splits ragweed out as its own category for exactly this reason.

Where ragweed is worst

Ragweed is far heavier in the eastern and midwestern US than on the Pacific coast. In our own August 2026 sampling of Google's per-plant ragweed index, the Great Plains and Midwest ran highest, while South Florida ran lowest — matching the well-documented regional pattern.

How to track it day to day

  1. Follow the plant, not the total. A combined index can look calm while ragweed specifically is climbing.
  2. Get warned in advance. Antihistamines generally work better started before exposure than after symptoms begin.
  3. Mornings are usually worse. Many wind-pollinated plants release in the morning.
  4. Rain helps, wind hurts. Rain scavenges grains out of the air; dry windy days lift and carry them.
